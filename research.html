Research

Tracing the path from multilingual experience to spoken words.

I study how the brain navigates competing languages—and how each speaker’s history changes that process.

Core question

When several languages are active at once, what allows a speaker to select the intended word—and what happens when that system fails?
Current directionEEG · Behavioral

Developmental Language Disorder and the energy trade-off hypothesis

This work extends theories on developmental language disorder trajectories and how the trade-off of energy between the body and brain can contribute to these trajectories.

Master’s researchBehavioral · Modeling

Age of acquisition and proficiency in trilingual word production

My master’s project examined 69 English–French–German trilingual speakers completing a speeded picture-naming task. Across more than 34,000 observations, I modeled vocal response times and cross-language intrusions in switch and non-switch contexts.

The results suggest that age of acquisition and proficiency do not act as interchangeable measures of language experience. Their effects depend on the target language, the previously used language, and the relative accessibility of competing words.

Looking ahead

Future directions

I hope to build an account of multilingual language control that respects both its shared neural architecture and the diversity of individual language histories. Longer term, I am interested in naturalistic speech, conversational neurophysiology, and the clinical relevance of multilingual brain organization.
